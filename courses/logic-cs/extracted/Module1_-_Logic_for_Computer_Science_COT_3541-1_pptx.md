## Slide 6: Propositional logic
- It is possible to see whether any provided statement is a proposition by prefixing it with:
  - It is true that …
- And find whether the result makes grammatical sense or not.
- Examples
  - It is true that 3 + 6 = 9. (it makes grammatical sense)
  - It is true that the weather is always cold at the equator. (it makes grammatical sense)
  - It is true that 18 – 9. (it does not make grammatical sense)

## Slide 7: Atomic propositions
- An atomic proposition is a proposition whose truth or falsity does not rely on the truth or falsity of any other proposition.
- As a result, all the earlier propositions mentioned in the previous slides are atomic.

## Slide 8: Propositional logic connectives
- Negation: A sentence such as ¬P (~P) is called the negation of P.
- Example:
  - Kiavash teaches AI this semester. (P)
  - Kiavash does not teach AI this semester. (¬P)
- Conjunction: A sentence with an ∧ connective such as P ∧ Q is called a conjunction.
- Example:
  - Kiavash is a professor of computer science, and he teaches the logic for computer science course. These two sentences can be written as:
    - Kiavash is a professor of computer science. (P)
    - Kiavash teaches the logic for computer science course. (Q)
    - Therefore: We will write P ∧ Q to conjunct these two sentences.

---
## Slide 9: Propositional logic connectives
- Disjunction: A sentence with an ∨ connective, such as P ∨ Q, is called disjunction, where P and Q are the propositions.
- Example: "Kiavash is a professor or a scientist“.
- As a result, "Kiavash is a professor" equals P.
- "Kiavash is a scientist" equals Q.
- Therefore, we can write the sentences as P ∨ Q.

---
## Slide 10: Propositional logic connectives
- Implication: A sentence like P → Q is called an implication. We also describe them as if-then rules.
- The implication can be described as follows:
  - If the car starts, then the car has fuel.
  - So, if P equals "the car starts", and Q equals "the car has fuel", it is defined as P → Q

---
## Slide 11: Propositional logic connectives
- Biconditional (if and only if) (iff): A sentence like P ⇔ Q is a biconditional sentence.
- Example: If I am a millionaire, then I have at least one million dollars.
- Therefore, P equals "If I am a millionaire."
- Q equals "I have at least one million dollars."
- Therefore, the sentence can be written as P ⇔ Q.
- Remember: The symbol ⇔ is the preferred shorthand for “P if and only if Q"
