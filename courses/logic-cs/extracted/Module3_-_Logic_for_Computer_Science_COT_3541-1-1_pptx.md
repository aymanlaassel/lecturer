## Slide 5: Inverse, converse, and contra-positive
- Example
  - If you have enough money, then you will buy a new house.
  - “You have enough money” is called the hypothesis, p.
  - And, “You will buy a new house” is called the conclusion, q.
- Inverse of the conditional sentence mentioned above:
  - Is the negation of both the hypothesis and the conclusion
  - If the statement is “If p, then q”
  - Then the inverse will be “If not p, then not q”
  - So, the inverse of p → q will be ¬p → ¬q
  - As a result, the inverse of “If you have enough money, then you will buy a new house.” will be “If you do not have enough money, then you will not buy a new house.”

## Slide 6: Inverse, converse, and contra-positive
- Example
  - If you have enough money, then you will buy a new house.
  - “You have enough money” is called the hypothesis, p.
  - And, “You will buy a new house” is called the conclusion, q.
- Converse of the conditional sentence mentioned above:
  - Is created by interchanging the hypothesis and the conclusion.
  - If the statement is “If p, then q”
  - Then the converse will be “If q, then p”
  - So, the converse of p → q will be q → p
  - As a result, the converse of “If you have enough money, then you will buy a new house.” will be “If you buy a new house, then you have enough money.”

## Slide 7: Inverse, converse, and contra-positive
- Example
  - If you have enough money, then you will buy a new house.
  - “You have enough money” is called the hypothesis, p.
  - And, “You will buy a new house” is called the conclusion, q.
- Contra-positive of the conditional sentence mentioned above:
  - Is created by interchanging the hypothesis and the conclusion of the inverse statement.
  - If the statement is “If p, then q”
  - Then the contra-positive will be “If not q, then not p”
  - So, the inverse of p → q will be ¬p → ¬q
  - Therefore, the contra-positive of p → q will be ¬q → ¬p
  - As a result, the contra-positive of “If you have enough money, then you will buy a new house.” will be “If you do not buy a new house, then you did not have enough money.”

## Slide 8: Inverse, converse, and contra-positive
- In a nutshell
- Conditional sentence: If you have enough money, then you will buy a new house. p → q

## Slide 9: Duality principle
- For any TRUE statement
  - The dual statement achieved by interchanging unions into intersections and vice versa.
  - And, interchanging Universal set into NULL set and vice versa
  - Will be TRUE
  - Hint:  If dual of any statement is the statement itself, it is said self-dual statement.
  - Example:
    - The Dual of (P ∩ Q) ∪ R will be (P ∪ Q) ∩ R

## Slide 11: Normal forms
- Conjunctive normal form
  - If we put disjunctive clauses together with ∧ or ∩, it is called conjunctive normal form.
  - Examples:
    - (P ∨ Q) ∧ (P ∨ R) ∧ (Q ∨ R ∨ S)
    - (P ∪ Q) ∩ (Q ∪ R)
    - (P ∨ R) ∧ (¬Q ∨ ¬R) ∧ Q
    - (S ∪ P) ∩ (¬P ∪ R) ∩ (Q ∪ ¬R) ∩ Q

---
## Slide 12: Normal forms
- Disjunctive normal form
  - If we put conjunctive clauses together with ∨ or ∪, it is called disjunctive normal form.
  - Examples:
    - (P ∧ Q) ∨ (P ∧ R) ∨ (Q ∧ R ∧ S)
    - (P ∩ Q) ∪ (Q ∩ R)
    - (P ∧ R) ∨ (¬Q ∧ ¬R) ∨ Q
    - (S ∩ P) ∪ (¬P ∩ R) ∪ (Q ∩ ¬R) ∪ Q

---
## Slide 13: Limitations of propositional normal forms
- Limitations of propositional logic
  - It is impossible to represent relations such as all, some, or none with propositional logic.
  - Example:
    - All vehicles have four wheels.
    - Some fruits are bitter.
    - None of the planets have water.
  - We cannot describe statements in terms of their properties or logical relationships.
